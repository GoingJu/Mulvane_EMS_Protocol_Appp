Introduction: 
 
These protocols have been designed to maximize the interventions appropriate for each level of care, decrease potential 
risks to the patient, and improve patient outcomes. 
 
The color-coded format will serve to allow each EMS professional to easily follow the potential interventions for their level 
of care.  
 
EMR 
 
• 
EMR, EMT, AEMT, and Paramedic standing orders. 
EMT 
 
• 
EMT, AEMT, and Paramedic standing orders. 
AEMT 
 
• 
AEMT and Paramedic standing orders. 
PARAMEDIC 
 
• 
Paramedic standing orders. 
PHYSICIAN OPTIONS 
 
• 
Any order within the level of care for the provider. 
Key Points/Considerations 
• 
Additional points specific to patients that fall within the protocol. 
 
BLS should be done before ALS, and advanced providers are responsible for all appropriate basic interventions. At all 
provider levels, the standing orders are highlighted. 
 
EMRs and EMTs are only authorized to administer medications listed in the KBEMS approved medication list of April 5, 
2019 if they have received authorized training from Mulvane EMS and have successfully completed credentialing process. 
 
It is the provider’s responsibility and duty to inform the physician of their level of training, and to decline any order that 
they feel is inappropriate for that level of training. 
 
Hopefully, these protocols will make your job easier and provide greater assistance with patient care.

Patient Confidentiality: 
 
Patient is defined as an individual who requests or has the potential of needing medical evaluation and/or treatment. 
 
104-191 Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 regulates the protection of information obtained 
when responding, evaluating, treating, and transporting patients. It is imperative that we maintain the confidence of 
patient information and ensure its security. Mulvane EMS prohibits the release of any patient information to anyone 
outside the organization unless required for purposes of treatment, payment, or health care operations. 
 
Every effort should be made to ensure patient information is not left open in public view or unattended.  
 
 
Legal Consent: 
 
• 
Informed Consent:  Originates from the legal and ethical right the patient has to direct what happens to his/her 
body. A conscious patient must be told in a manner he/she can understand, the nature and extent of the 
procedure to be performed and the possible risks involved. If consent is refused by a conscious and mentally 
competent adult for any treatment procedure following explanation, that person cannot be treated.  
 
• 
Implied Consent:  Happens when you are unable to communicate with the patient. Most commonly, this is 
because the patient is unconscious. When a patient cannot express his or her wishes, there is an assumption that 
he/she would ask to be treated at the scene and transported to a medical facility where emergency life-saving 
treatment may be given. 
 
• 
Minor Consent:  The age of majority in Kansas is 18 years of age. To administer care to a minor requires parental 
or guardian consent (either in person or by telephone). If a responsible person is not available, emergency 
treatment to sustain life and alleviate pain and suffering may be undertaken without consent.  
 
Refusal of Treatment: 
 
It is the policy of Mulvane EMS to give the competent adult patient the right to refuse examination, treatment and/or 
transport for any illness or injury. 
 
The following guidelines must be followed to allow a patient to be released: 
 
• 
The patient MUST be competent and rational to make the decision to refuse service. 
 
• 
The patient MUST be 18 years of age or have written documentation of emancipation.  
 
• 
The patient must be aware of complications that could arise from the decision to not be transported.

Procedure when a Physician is on the Scene: 
For situations in which a physician is on the scene, the following procedure should be followed; such situations are divided 
into two types for the purpose of this procedure.   
 
Routine, Normal Situations 
 
• 
Conduct yourself in a professional demeanor and respectful attitude at all times. 
• 
Inquire if the physician is the patient’s regular physician. If so, consult the physician as to the patient’s history, 
prior treatment, etc.  
• 
Advise the physician that we have medical standing protocols established by our Medical Director and request 
that we are allowed to continue under these standing orders. 
• 
Follow any reasonable request by the physician. 
• 
Verbal orders received from in-town physicians and/or practitioners may be followed without them riding in 
to the hospital with the proper documentation.  
 
Problem or Crisis Situation 
 
• 
Follow the steps outlined above. 
• 
If the physician prefers to assume responsibility for the patient, the following requirements must be satisfied. 
 
o 
The Physician must provide proof of Kansas licensure. 
o 
The Physician must accompany the patient to the medical facility in our unit. 
o 
The physician must initiate any procedure that is contrary to our protocols. 
 
• 
If, in the opinion of the charge technician, the patient’s well-being is endangered or if the physician does not 
agree to our procedure in its entirety, the receiving Emergency Room Physician shall be contacted. 
 
 
Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) Directives and Orders: 
 
The following guidelines will help when a DNR situation is encountered. 
 
• 
If the care provider believes a DNR order exists, attempt to locate the order while continuing with appropriate 
care. 
 
o 
If a valid DNR, Directive, or Order is produced at any point during a resuscitative effort, all resuscitative 
efforts should be stopped. 
o 
A Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) Directive or Order may be honored if ANY of the following conditions are met: 
 
➢ 
A valid pre-hospital directive or authorization by an attorney in fact named in a valid Durable Power 
of Attorney for Health Care Decisions. 
➢ 
A physician’s order to “Do Not Resuscitate”.  
➢ 
A DNR Medic Alert identifier (bracelet or necklace). 
 
Terminally ill patients who have a Living Will or TPOPP/POLST (Transportable Physician Orders for Patient 
Preferences/Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) may be treated per the provisions of these documents 
instead of Mulvane EMS Standing Orders or Protocols. In the absence of one of these valid documents, terminally ill 
patients may be transported without oxygen, IV fluids or medications at the request of a lucid patient or an attorney in 
fact named in a valid Durable Power of Attorney for Health Care Decisions.

Infection Control and Exposures: 
 
It is everyone’s duty to protect themselves, their crew, and the patients from infection and exposures.  By simple measures 
all personnel and patients can be exposed to hazards.  The following are precautions to help eliminate those exposures. 
 
• 
At the beginning of every shift the crew will decontaminate the truck.  This is to include the entire back and front 
passenger compartments and equipment. 
   
• 
Immediately after every call: 
 
o 
Linens will be replaced. 
o 
Equipment wiped down. 
o 
Counters wiped down. 
 
• 
Once each month each truck will have the cabinets emptied and thoroughly cleaned.  This is to include the back, 
outside compartments, and the front passenger compartment. 
 
• 
All sharps containers should be used for needles and sharp objects only.  Do not fill past the recommended fill 
height.  Any full containers will be properly disposed of and replaced with new ones. 
 
• 
Do not eat, drink, apply makeup, or smoke as these will increase the risk of an exposure. 
 
• 
Anytime patient contact is made, the provider should take universal precautions to reduce the risk of exposures.  
These key tips will help minimize exposures: 
 
o 
Wear non-latex gloves when there is a possibility of contact with any bodily fluids. 
o 
Wear eye protection and gown whenever there is a splash hazard. 
o 
Washing hands after every call is the easiest way to decrease exposure. 
o 
During vehicle extrication the crew will wear the provided protective clothing inside the work area 
established by the fire department. 
o 
A safety vest will be worn on all scenes on a roadway. 
 
• 
If an exposure occurs contact one of the following: 
 
o 
EMS Lieutenant 
o 
EMS Captain 
o 
Public Safety Chief   
 
• 
If the exposure is in the eyes or mucous membrane the area should be flushed for at least 15 minutes.  All 
other exposures should be washed with water and a non-abrasive antibacterial soap.  Lastly, an exposure 
report will be filled out and will remain on file for no less than 40 years.

Quality Assurance: 
The role of Quality Assurance is to evaluate the quality of care provided to patients requesting service from Mulvane 
EMS. Quality Assurance is comprised of multiple quality control activities. These activities exist within our organization 
to provide consistent quality care, and to evaluate existing policies or procedures. Moreover, quality control activities 
exist to improve the quality of care provided and enhance the EMS system’s responsiveness to the citizens and visitors 
of Mulvane and the surrounding area. 
Quality Assurance is a structured and functional review of quality control activities and provides feedback to the 
involved individuals, which can effectuate desirable change. The approval of these Protocols and Standing Orders is 
contingent on the continued operation of a Quality Assurance Program and periodic (quarterly) reporting of the results 
of the process must be made to the Medical Director of Mulvane EMS. 
Definitions and Components 
• 
Protocol: Establishes the standards to be achieved under specific circumstances within the EMS system. 
Guidelines contained herein provide conditions, restrictions, or requirements when accomplishing medical 
procedures or treatment regimes. Protocols are useful in teaching and provide a standard by which care provided 
can be reviewed by the service Medical Director. 
• 
Standing Order: Provides legal authority to carry out procedures on behalf of the physician(s) who has signed 
them. Additionally, they authorize treatment before radio contact is made. Usually these orders provide 
treatment of time urgent situations, i.e. cardiac arrest. 
• 
On-Line Medical Control: Provides direct real-time physician to pre-hospital provider communication. Once 
contact is made, the pre-hospital provider becomes an agent of the on-line physician and carries out orders given. 
• 
Off-Line Medical Control:  Provides medical direction prior to or upon review of the actual real time emergency. 
Included in off-line medical control are: Protocols, Standing Orders and Call Critiques. Compliance by EMS 
personnel with Protocol and Standing Orders is evaluated by an audit and review process which is supervised by 
the Medical Director.  
• 
Medical Director: A person licensed to practice medicine and surgery in the State of Kansas. The Medical Director 
is responsible for Educational Standards, Protocol Development, Critique, Planning and Quality Assurance. Active 
involvement in off-line medical control is essential. 
Procedures for Quality Control 
• 
Off-Line Activities: 
o 
Chart Review: Charts written by field personnel are audited to evaluate compliance with departmental 
Protocol and Standing Orders. 
• 
On-Line Activities: 
o 
Medical Control: Each time medical orders, beyond Standing Orders are anticipated, a medical control 
physician is to be contacted. The seven local medical control facilities are: 
 Via-Christi St. Francis Regional Medical Center 
 Via-Christi St. Joseph Regional Medical Center 
 Via-Christ Wellington ER 
 Wesley Medical Center 
 Wesley Woodlawn Hospital 
 Wesley Derby  
 Rock Regional Medical Center 
 Robert J Dole Veteran’s Hospital 
These constitute the majority of receiving facilities for Mulvane emergency patients. The medical control physicians should 
be contacted during unusual occurrences or situations that require special attention. These orders require the following 
stipulations to be documented on the chart by the technician: 
 The name of the Physician that gave the order; The verbatim content of the “Verbal Order.”; The signature 
of the Physician giving the order.

Patient Transport/Destination: 
 
The following guidelines provide general rules for the establishment of hospital destinations for patients transported by 
Mulvane EMS. 
 
Non-routine factors, such as poor road or weather conditions, diversion status of hospitals, number of patients requiring 
transport, etc., may alter how these guidelines are followed. 
 
Approved Hospital Destinations 
• 
Approved receiving hospital destinations for Mulvane EMS are: 
• 
Rock Regional Hospital 
• 
Wesley Derby ER 
• 
Wesley Main 
• 
Wesley Woodlawn 
• 
Via Christi St. Joseph 
• 
Via Christi St. Francis 
• 
Via Christi Wellington (Code Green and Orange only) 
 
Emergency Transports 
 
• 
Non-Critical Patients: (Code Green) will be transported to either Rock Regional Hospital, Wesley Derby ER and Via 
Christi Wellington ER. The exception is VA patients, institutional calls or pre-arranged transfers.  
• 
Workman’s Compensation situations may allow the employer to determine hospital destination. 
• 
Patients in police custody may be transported to a destination selected by the arresting officer. 
 
• 
Code Orange Patients:  Should be transported to the area hospital of the patient’s preference unless a legal 
guardian, spouse, or law enforcement officer requests an alternate facility. Any ER can assess/treat patients with 
psychiatric/mental health complaints. 
  
• 
Emergency Critical Patients:  (Code Red or Blue) will be transported to the appropriate facility of the patient 
preference or as directed by a spouse or legal guardian, VA excluded.  If a patient cannot or will not make such a 
choice, transport will be made to the closest appropriate Wichita hospital facility; VA excluded.   
 
• 
Deceased Patients (Code Black): Will not be routinely transported by EMS. Only following a “coroner’s release” 
will a patient be moved or transported to the morgue of the coroner’s choice. Normally, transport of deceased 
patients is requested by law enforcement personnel at the scene when the body is in public view. 
 
• 
Isolated Carbon Monoxide Poisonings:  Transport to Wesley Medical Center for Hyperbaric Oxygen (HBO) 
treatment. If the etiology is uncertain, follow the most appropriate transport guideline. 
 
Burn Alert Patients: 
 
• 
Burn Alert Patients:  Transport only to Via Christi St. Francis.  
 
❖ Note: Code Blue Burned OB (with or without trauma) shall be transported to Wesley Medical Center. 
 
STEMI Alert Patients: 
 
• 
STEMI Alerts:  Transport will be to Wesley Medical Center, Via Christi St. Francis, or Kansas Medical Center. 
 
• 
Code Blue/Post Code Blue:   Transport only to Via-Christi St. Francis or Wesley Medical Center.

General Assessment: 
  
The General Assessment procedure will be used for all patients and should be completed for every patient that the 
provider encounters. 
 
Patient Definition 
 
• 
Patient Criteria:  An assessment, history, and offer of transport will be performed and documented.  
 
o 
Request for EMS by him/herself. 
o 
Apparent illness or injury. 
o 
Has a Chief Complaint. 
o 
Vital signs are obtained. (Exception: walk-in request for blood pressure check without complaint of illness or 
injury.) 
 
• 
No Patient Criteria:  
 
o 
Accidental medical alarm activation. 
o 
No person on scene. 
o 
MVC victim with no apparent injury or complaint. 
 
• 
Citizen Assist Criteria: 
 
o 
Request for assistance with medical equipment who has no acute illness, injury, or complaint. (Example: 
changing an oxygen cylinder.) 
o 
Lift assistance with no injury or illness causing the fall, no complaint of injury, and no vital signs assessed. 
Scene Size-up 
 
• 
Scene Safety:  Paramount.  All providers will make a scene assessment upon arrival to determine any unsafe scene 
or patient.  If the scene is unsafe, providers will standby until the scene is secured by proper personnel. 
 
• 
Body Substance Isolation (BSI):  All personnel will take proper precautionary measures when making contact with 
patients (i.e. gloves, glasses, gowns, and masks.)  Providers should also wash hands after calls, as hand washing is 
one of the best ways to prevent spreading diseases. 
  
• 
Mechanism of injury (MOI) / Nature of illness (NOI):   MOI is the event that caused the injury.  On-scene size up 
should indicate potential injuries.  NOI is the patient’s reason/chief complaint for calling EMS. 
 
• 
Number of patients and triage:   It is crucial that the number of patients is ascertained.  If there are more than 
two patients, additional resources may be needed and/or a need to establish an Incident Command System.  
Triage is the condition of the patient. 
 
• 
Consider additional resources:   Additional resources should be requested as needed.

Initial Assessment 
 
Complete as indicated. Scene time should be kept to a minimum. A Rapid Trauma Assessment and a detailed physical 
exam should be done on traumas.  A focused history should be done on medicals. 
 
• 
Determine level of consciousness.  
• 
Assess circulation, airway, and breathing. 
• 
Perform a Quick head-to-toe assessment. 
• 
Identify any life-threatening injuries or illness and treat. 
• 
Obtain and record vital signs, including: peripheral pulse (rate, regularity and quality), blood pressure, respirations 
(character and rate), skin (condition, temperature and color), breath sounds, motor function and pupils. This 
should be done a minimum of every fifteen (15) minutes or more frequently as indicated by patient condition. 
• 
Obtain a SAMPLE history.  Signs/symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Prior medical history, Last oral intake, Events 
leading up to, and Interventions.  This can be information from the patient, family and/or bystanders. 
Detailed Physical Exam 
 
A Detailed Physical Exam should be completed on all trauma patients.  When performing a detailed physical exam, utilize 
DCAP-BTLS.  Deformities, Contusions, Abrasions, Punctures/Penetrations, Burns, Tenderness, Lacerations, and Swelling.  
A detailed physical exam should examine the following: 
 
• 
Head, Neck, and C-Spine:  Skull, face, eyes, mouth, ears, nose, and throat 
• 
Chest:  Breath sounds (Rate, rhythm, and quality), crepitus, and bruising 
• 
Abdomen:  Soft, firm, tender, distended, and guarding 
• 
Pelvis:   Stability, pain, and tenderness 
• 
Extremities:   Distal circulation, sensation, and motor 
• 
Back:  Stability and incontinence 
Focused History 
 
When warranted a focused history should be performed and included as a part of the SAMPLE history. OPQRSTI will 
assist with cardiac assessments. OPQRSTI is as follows: 
 
• 
Onset:  When did it start? 
• 
Provokes:  What makes it better or worse? 
• 
Quality:  What does the pain feel like? 
• 
Radiates:  Does it go anywhere else on your body? 
• 
Severity:   On a scale of 1-10, rate the pain? 
• 
Timing:   Is pain constant or does it come and go? 
• 
Interventions:  What have they done or taken to help? 
 
❖ Note: If the patient is unresponsive a rapid assessment should be performed. 
Field Treatments 
 
Treat appropriately in order of priority.

Oxygen Administration 
 
Oxygen administration may be applied by all levels of providers. Based on patient need and/or protocol, supplemental 
oxygen will be administered to maintain a saturation level above 94% with the appropriate delivery device determined by 
the technician. 
 
Blood Glucose 
 
Blood glucose will be assessed on all patients that have an altered level of consciousness, suspected hyper/hypoglycemia, 
unknown etiology, ETOH suspicion, overdoses, seizures and at the discretion of the technician.  All patients that are being 
treated for diabetic emergencies need to have blood glucose levels taken every 15 minutes or whenever a treatment is

Stroke Scale Assessment: 
 
The Cincinnati Pre-hospital Stroke Scale is a system used to diagnose the presence of a stroke in a patient. It tests three 
signs for abnormal findings which may indicate that the patient is having a stroke. If any one of the three tests shows 
abnormal findings, the patient may be having a stroke and should be transported to a hospital as soon as possible. 
• 
Facial droop: Have the person smile or show his/her teeth. If one side doesn't move as well as the other and it 
seems to droop, that could be sign of a stroke. 
 
o 
Normal: Both sides of face move equally. 
o 
Abnormal: One side of face does not move as well as the other (or not at all). 
 
• 
Arm drift: Have the person close his/her eyes and hold his/her arms straight out in front for about 10 seconds. If 
one arm does not move, or one arm winds up drifting down more than the other, that could be a sign of a stroke. 
• 
Normal:  Both arms move equally or not at all. 
o 
Abnormal:  One arm does not move or one arm drifts down compared with the other side. 
 
• 
Speech:  Have the person say, "You can't teach an old dog new tricks," or some other simple, familiar saying. If the 
person slurs the words, gets some words wrong, or is unable to speak, that could be a sign of stroke. 
 
o 
Normal:  Patient uses correct words with no slurring. 
o 
Abnormal:  Slurred or inappropriate words or mute. 
NOTE: If a family member or the witnessing bystander does not accompany EMS to the hospital, get that person’s phone 
number for the hospital to contact him/her with any further questions about the patient.

secured in place assuring that it will not fall against the door or other equipment in the compartment.  
 
❖ Note:  If the firearm is in its holster, leave it in the holster.  
❖ Note: The firearm can be left at the patient’s residence.

Automatic External Defibrillator: 
 
The Automatic External Defibrillator (AED) may only be used on an unconscious, pulseless, and apneic patient.   
 
Indication 
 
• 
The patient must weigh 90 pounds and/or have a large enough physical stature that the patches have ample 
space to be properly placed on the body. 
• 
Insure that the patient is unconscious, pulseless, and apneic. 
• 
Do not apply to patients that have a valid DNR, DPO, or Living Will that indicate that the patient does not want 
to be resuscitated. 
• 
Follow the manufacturer’s recommendations for adult and pediatric placement of patches and size of patches. 
Precautions 
 
• 
Do not use the AED in explosive or hazardous environments. 
• 
Do not touch the patient or electrodes while the AED is analyzing or defibrillating. 
• 
Do not place the patches directly over an implanted defibrillator or pacemaker. 
• 
The patient must have a patent airway or the use of the AED will not be productive. 
Procedure 
 
• 
Determine that the patient is unconscious, pulseless, and apneic. 
• 
If the arrest is unwitnessed CPR must be started for no less than two minutes. 
• 
Apply the appropriately sized patches to the patient in the sterna and apex positions (shown on the patches). 
• 
Turn on the AED and ANALYZE without touching the patient, AED, or the cables. 
• 
If the AED advises “SHOCK INDICATED”, at that time the patient must be cleared of all providers and only then 
can the SHOCK button be pressed. 
• 
Resume CPR and ventilations with a BVM for two minutes and then the pulse should be checked. 
• 
If the AED advises “NO SHOCK INDICATED”, CPR must be immediately started. 
• 
Resume CPR and ventilations with a BVM for two minutes and then the pulse should be checked. 
Documentation 
 
• 
The application, number of shocks, and the results of the patient must be properly recorded and documented in 
the patient care report.

Chest Decompression/Needle Thoracentesis: 
 
A life-threatening situation may arise when a “one-way” valve is created that allows air to enter but not leave the pleural 
space. As the pressure in the pleural space exceeds the outside atmospheric pressure, the physiologic consequences of a 
simple pneumothorax are magnified to include tension pneumothorax. Increasing pressure within the pleural space 
further compromises pulmonary function/capacity and will force the mediastinum to the contra lateral side. This will result 
in increasing difficulty breathing (respirations) and a decrease in blood flow to the heart. As the pleural space increases, 
the mediastinum shifts and will result in a high venous pressure caused by the kinking of the Vena Cava. This causes a 
reduced blood flow into the heart and decreased cardiac output.  
 
Signs and Symptoms 
  
• 
Extreme anxiety, cyanosis, tachypnea, diminished or absent breath sounds to the injured hemisphere, bulging of 
the intercostal muscles, hyper-resonance, jugular vein distention, tachycardia, narrowing pulse pressures, 
hypotension, subcutaneous emphysema, and tracheal deviation (if seen -  a late sign). 
 
• 
Cyanosis and decreased breath sounds are the most common early findings with a pneumothorax. Other signs and 
symptoms develop later as pressure increases. Tachycardia and increased dyspnea despite treatment may be 
obvious later. JVD and tracheal deviation are among the latest of the signs and symptoms. 
 
Authorized Use 
 
• 
For relief of tension pneumothorax by standing order. 
• 
For relief of pneumothorax by physician order.  
Insertion site 
 
• 
Second intercostal space 
• 
Mid-clavicular line or axillary line if MCL site does not relieve pneumothorax, unable to access MCL, or if patient is 
obese. 
• 
Same side as injury 
Contraindications 
• 
None  
 
Technique 
• 
Cleanse the site with betadine. 
• 
Twist off the red cap and remove the 14 ga needle and catheter 
• 
Insert at a 90-degree angle to the chest wall into the intercostal space over the superior border of the third rib, 
midclavicular line. If using anterior axillary site, insert at 90 degree angle to the chest wall into the 5th intercostal 
space over the superior border of the 6th rib, approximately a hands width from the axilla. 
• 
Insert into the pleural space while listening for the sudden escape of air. 
• 
Remove the needle portion and leave the catheter in place. 
• 
Secure to the chest with a chest seal securing it adhesive side down with the valve directly over the catheter. 
• 
Press dressing firmly in place to assure an occlusive seal. 
• 
Re-evaluate breath sounds and vital signs. 
• 
Monitor closely for recurrence of respiratory distress. 
PEDIATRIC TECHNIQUE 
• 
Insertion site is 2nd ICS mid-clavicular line ONLY 
• 
Ages 0-1 years: use 22g IV needle 
• 
Ages 1-4 years: use 20g IV needle

Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP): 
Continuous Positive Airway Pressure has been shown to rapidly improve vital signs, gas exchange, reduce the work of 
breathing, decrease the sense of dyspnea, and decrease the need for endotracheal intubation in patients who suffer from 
shortness of breath from asthma, COPD, pulmonary edema, CHF, and pneumonia. In patients with CHF, CPAP improves 
hemodynamics by reducing left ventricular preload and afterload. 
Indications 
Any patient who is in respiratory distress with signs and symptoms consistent with asthma, COPD, pulmonary edema, CHF, 
or pneumonia and who is: 
• 
Awake and able to follow commands. 
• 
Is over 8 years old and is able to fit the CPAP mask. 
• 
Has the ability to maintain an open airway. 
• 
And exhibits two or more of the following: 
o 
A respiratory rate greater than 25 breaths per minute. 
o 
SPO2 of less than 94% at any time. 
o 
Use of accessory muscles during respirations. 
Contraindications 
• 
Patient is in respiratory arrest/apneic. 
• 
Patient is suspected of having a pneumothorax or has suffered chest trauma. 
• 
Patient has a tracheostomy. 
• 
Patient is actively vomiting or has upper GI bleeding. 
Procedure 
• 
Explain the procedure to the patient. 
• 
Ensure adequate oxygen supply to ventilation device. 
• 
Place the patient on continuous pulse oximetry. 
• 
Place the patient on cardiac monitor and record rhythm strip. 
• 
Place the patient on EtCO2 monitoring via the nasal cannula before placing the mask but DO NOT break the seal 
to apply the cannula. 
• 
Place the delivery device over the mouth and nose. 
• 
Secure the mask with provided straps or other provided devices. 
• 
For CHF/Pulmonary Edema, titrate to 10 cm/H2O. For all other asthma & COPD patients titrate to 2-5 cm H2O. 
• 
Check for air leaks.

Intramuscular Medication (IM) Administration: 
Drugs administered by the intramuscular (IM) route are deposited into vascular muscle tissue, which allows for rapid 
absorption into the circulation. 
Indications: 
When medication administration is necessary, and the medication must be given via IM route or as an alternative route in 
selected medications. 
 
Contraindications 
• 
Active infection, cellulitis, or dermatitis at the site of administration 
• 
Known allergy or hypersensitivity to the drug 
• 
Acute myocardial infarction- the release of muscle enzymes may provide a confounding bias in making the 
diagnosis 
• 
Thrombocytopenia 
• 
Coagulation defects 
• 
Hypovolemic shock- the absorption of the drug may be hampered owing to compromised vascularity to the 
muscle 
 
Procedure 
• 
Explain the procedure and gain consent. 
• 
Before drug administration, check whether the patient has any allergies. 
• 
Check the prescription is correct, following the ‘five rights’ of drug administration to reduce the risk of error. 
• 
Assemble the syringe and needle and withdraw the required amount of drug from the ampoule. Some 
medicines are available in pre-filled syringes and manufacturer’s instructions should be followed. 
• 
Disperse air bubbles from the syringe. 
• 
Expose the selected area and cleanse the injection site with alcohol. 
• 
Insert the needle into the skin with a smooth, steady motion 
• 
90-degree angle skin pinched skin flattened 
• 
Aspirate for blood 
• 
 Inject the medication. 
• 
 Withdraw the needle quickly and dispose of properly without recapping. 
• 
Apply pressure to the site. 
• 
Apply band aid 
• 
 Monitor the patient for the desired therapeutic effects as well as any possible side effects. 
• 
 Document the medication, dose, route, and time on/with the patient care report (PCR).

Taser Dart Removal and Care: 
 
A taser works by firing two wire-attached darts that can strike a suspect from up to 15 feet or more. It delivers up to 
50,000 volts of electricity to the recipient. This jolt of electricity is not harmful to vital body functions such as heart rhythm, 
pacemaker function or respirations.  The taser dart usually penetrates the skin only a few millimeters. Technicians can 
safely remove a dart simply by pulling it out. The only exception is involvement of the eye, face, neck, breast, groin, spinal 
column or joint. In this case, leave the dart in place and transport the patient to the hospital for dart removal.  Strongly 
consider scene safety and measures to protect yourself and other rescuers from a potentially violent patient in situations 
when a taser gun has been used. You do not need to transport a person to the hospital based solely on taser dart exposure. 
If a patient has no need for further medical evaluation, they can be left in police custody.   
 
• 
Scene Safety. 
• 
Confirm that the taser has been shut off and the probe is no longer connected to the taser gun. 
• 
Obtain vital signs at the earliest opportunity. Violent and combative behavior may be secondary to intoxication, 
psychosis, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, OD or CNS infection. Treat trauma and seizure as applicable. 
• 
Evaluate the anatomical location of the probe(s) puncture zone(s). High-risk/sensitive zones will require transport 
to a medical facility for removal. They Include:  
 
o 
Eyes, ears, nose, mouth and neck. (Darts to scalp, and low risk areas of forehead and cheek, can be removed 
in the field, but these wounds may require assessment by a physician). 
o 
Breasts (female) 
o 
Genitalia 
o 
Hands or Feet 
o 
Joints   
o 
Spinal column 
 
Removal Procedure 
 
• 
Utilize PPE.  
• 
Prior to probe removal inform all caregivers that you are about to remove the contaminated sharp. 
• 
Place hand in the form of a “V” around the taser dart in order to stabilize the surrounding skin and to keep loose 
skin from coming up with the dart. Firmly grasp the probe and with one smooth hard jerk, remove probe from 
skin. (remove probes one at a time)  
• 
Examine the probe and the patient closely in an effort to make sure the probe tip did not break off during removal. 
Accordingly, it is important that the person removing the barb visually inspect it to make sure that the tip is fully 
intact. If the barb remains in the subject, the patient will need to be transported to a medical facility for barb 
removal.  
• 
Be careful to avoid accidental needle sticks when removing probes.  
• 
Removed probes should be handled like contaminated sharps and should be placed in the specimen container 
provided by the PD. They will likely log the probes into evidence and 6 inches of the wire must remain intact with 
the taser buds if the patient is transported.  
• 
Provide wound care by cleansing the affected area with saline and apply a band aid. 
• 
Inform patient of basic wound care and the need to seek additional care in the event that signs of infection occur 
(redness-fever-drainage-swelling etc.) 
• 
Clear and thorough documentation is required in the body of the report narrative whether or not EMS transports 
the patient.  
• 
If transport is necessary, transport to the closest appropriate hospital.

TOPICAL HEMOSTATIC AGENT APPLICATION, ADULT & PEDIATRIC 
 
Indications:  
o Life-threatening hemorrhage to junctional areas of the neck, axilla, and/or groin 
o Severe scalp lacerations w/ uncontrolled bleeding 
o Life-threatening hemorrhage to extremities unable to be controlled by direct pressure and/or 
tourniquet usage 
Contraindications: 
o Open thoracic, abdominal, and/or penetrating head wounds 
Background: 
QuikClot Combat Gauze is a sterile gauze (rolled or in 4x4 pads) impregnated with kaolin, a compound shown 
to increase clotting in severe hemorrhage by activating factor XII of the clotting cascade. Kaolin is a naturally 
occurring inorganic compound that has not been found to cause any allergic reactions. 
 
Application: 
1. Apply direct pressure to the wound and a tourniquet as applicable. 
2. Remove excess pooled blood from around the wound while preserving any clots already in the wound. 
3. For junctional and extremity injuries, pack as much rolled QuikClot gauze into the wound as possible and use it 
to apply pressure directly to the bleeding source until the bleeding stops. More than one package of hemostatic 
agent may be needed. 
a. For severe scalp lacerations: apply the 4x4 QuikClot gauze over the wound, DO NOT PACK, and apply 
direct pressure to the bleeding source until bleeding stops. More than one package may be needed. 
b. For adjunct therapy for extremity wounds uncontrolled by direct pressure and/or tourniquet, pack as 
much rolled QuikClot gauze into the wound as possible and use it to apply pressure directly to the 
bleeding source until the bleeding stops. 
4. Continue applying direct pressure to the wound for 3-5 minutes. 
5. Leave the hemostatic agent in place. Place empty package over wound to ensure it is transported w/ the patient 
to the destination facility.  
6. Dress the wound with a pressure dressing to secure the hemostatic agent. 
a. In the case of severe wounds to the neck, direct pressure may have to be continuously applied 
throughout transport. 
NOTE: It is extremely important that direct pressure is provided immediately upon application of the 
hemostatic agent and held for at least 3-5 minutes.
